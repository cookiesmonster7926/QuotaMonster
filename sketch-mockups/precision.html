Sleeping Gauge

Two bottom-open arcs of remaining quota, a 50% tick at top dead centre on each, the running agents as dots nested in the opening, and a creature in the middle that only moves while work is in flight — asleep when the quota is gone. Built as an instrument: 18pt of ink in the 22pt box, every edge on a whole device pixel, every value taken from the measured floors rather than from the eye.

1  Construction — 10×, on the device pixel grid

The alignment proof

Two 1× scanlines through the finished mark, sampled out of a real rasteriser — this page rendered headless at device‑scale 1 and the alpha read back per pixel. Straight edges on whole points come back binary: 1.00 ink, 0.00 gap, no exceptions. Curved strokes do not — a 1.0pt arc lands about 0.90 in its own pixel and bleeds 0.10–0.16 into one neighbour, which is exactly why the counters are 2.0 everywhere the ticks are not. Numbers below are measured, not estimated.

What 18pt of ink bought, and what it cost

The brief’s 16pt target holds for a single glyph. This composition is five concentric bands — arc, counter, arc, counter, creature — and at the measured floors (1.0 stroke, 2.0 counter) five bands need 9pt of radius exactly. At r = 8 the creature collapses to 4pt and stops being a creature. So the ink goes to 18×18 with 2.0pt margins, which is also Apple’s own template size, and the optical weight is checked against a real battery and wifi glyph in section 3.

Honest optical weight: the mark is the biggest thing on the bar. 18pt of height against the wifi fan’s 11 and the battery’s 10. Parity is what forces it — for the arc edges to land on whole pixels at top dead centre the ring diameter has to be even, so the only choices are 16 and 18, and at 16 the creature collapses to 4pt and stops being a creature. 18 wins on the design and loses on the neighbourly behaviour. The single surprise: a 1.0pt curved stroke is not binary even when its edges are on whole points. Skia lands it at ~0.90 in its own pixel and pushes 0.10–0.16 into one neighbour. Straight edges — ticks, dots, gaps, the eye slot — came back perfectly binary, every time. That asymmetry is the argument for 2.0pt counters and for putting the only fine detail in the mark, the 50% tick and the eye, on straight edges.

Retina‑only, stated plainly

  • Counting past five. The 1.0pt tier resolves better than expected — gaps measured a true 0.00 — but the marks land at 0.63–0.84 against the 2pt tier’s 0.97, so the row reads as a lighter texture rather than as countable units. Five is the honest ceiling at 1×. The exact number lives in the dropdown.
  • The creature’s squash. The 4.5% breath moves its edge by 0.13pt. At 1× that is alpha flicker on the boundary pixels — it reads as a shimmer, not a squash. The dots, not the creature, are the hard signal that agents are running.
  • The closed eye. It survives 1× as a real hole, 83% open — but at 6×4pt of body it reads as a notch. It becomes an eye at 2×.
  • The empty track on a light menu bar at 1×.
  • Curves. Only the four cardinal tangents of a circle can be grid-locked, and even there the measured alpha is 0.93, not 1.00. Everything the parity discipline buys is spent on the straight elements: ticks, dots, gaps, the eye slot, the lane. The 45° reaches of both arcs are antialiased at every scale factor and no geometry fixes it.

The one alignment assumption

The 1.0pt counters at the ticks are clean only while the image origin lands on a whole point. The mark is 22×22 — even — inside a 22pt bar, so AppKit centres it at an integer and the assumption holds. Ship it at an odd width, or centre it in an odd-height bar, and those two counters close to 25% fill. That is the single fragile dependency in the design and it is worth a unit test on the drawn rect.

7  What was given up

Deliberate sacrifices

  • Stroke weight. 1.0pt on 18pt of ink is 5.6%, under the 8–11% band. Five concentric bands in 9pt of radius cannot hold a 1.5pt stroke without closing a 2.0pt counter. Counters won, because a closed counter destroys the composition and a light stroke only looks light.
  • Ink height. 18pt, not the 16pt target. At 16pt the creature collapses to 4.0pt and stops reading as anything alive. 18×18 is Apple’s own template size, and section 3 checks the optical weight against real neighbours.
  • The count above five. Subitising gives out at four or five anyway; a lane that claims to encode eleven at 22pt is lying about its resolution.
  • Quota resolution. 1px of arc is 2.70% on the 5‑hour window and 4.17% on the 7‑day one. Sub-3% changes are invisible. The mark is a gauge, not a readout.
  • The alert hides the data. Sealing the ring is what makes the alert readable in monochrome, and it costs both arcs while it is up. Acceptable: needs-input is the only state with a deadline, and it is transient.
  • Blocked count. One blocked versus several is carried by ring weight, 2.0 to 3.0, plus the march rate. Beyond “more than one” the number is not in the mark.

Where the drawing was followed exactly

  • Two concentric arcs, both open at the bottom, both remaining quota that shrink as it is spent.
  • A tick on each arc at top dead centre. Because each arc starts in the bottom gap and sweeps over the top, TDC is the exact midpoint of the sweep — so the ticks are the 50% marks, as drawn.
  • Agent dots in the opening at the bottom, one per agent, status by colour and by alpha.
  • A creature in the middle that moves only while work is in flight.
  • The red morph: a sealed ring and three nested chevrons.
  • Quota gone: the pet lies down and sleeps inside an empty circle.

The layout answer

The dot row never stacks below the ring. It sits inside the 110° opening, in the lane the arcs themselves vacate, at y18–20 — so it costs nothing in height. That is the whole trick, and it is the user’s own drawing read literally. The ring’s bottom extremity is at y16.8 where the arcs terminate, the lane starts at y18, and the mark still fits 18pt of ink in 22 with 2pt of margin top and bottom.
