The mouth is where the lamps live

The sketch asks for five stacked bands inside 22 points: outer arc, gap, inner arc, gap, creature — and then a row of dots underneath. That last row is the impossible one. But the sketch already contains its own solution: both arcs are open at the bottom, so the ring never uses the bottom of its own box. The 96° mouth donates exactly the height the agent row needs, and it happens to hold exactly three lamps at zero extra width — the three the sketch draws. The fourth agent is what buys width: the row locks its origin inside the mouth and trails right, so the ring appears to be emitting the lamps rather than wearing a badge.

Four parallel strokes and a core, on a strict 1.50 module. That is the whole element budget for 18pt of ink, spent exactly once.

Why top dead centre is 50%

Each arc starts at 228° (bottom-left) and sweeps clockwise through 264° to 132° (bottom-right). Half of 264° is 132°, which lands on 0° — twelve o’clock. So the arc tip crossing the top of the mark is the fifty-percent event, and the tick only has to mark the place where it happens.

The tick had to become a notch

An added tick mark cannot coexist with 1.50pt counters — there is no radial room for a mark plus two gaps. So the tick is subtracted: a 1.00pt vertical slot at x 10.50–11.50 cutting both arcs. It costs zero radial budget, and because the arcs are horizontally tangent at the top, its two edges are vertical lines that snap to whole device pixels at every scale — the only place on a circle where that is true.

The payoff, and the price. The notch is visible only while the arc still reaches the top, so seeing the notch means more than half is left. Below 50% the reference mark is gone; you read the tip position instead.

Dot row capacity — the load-bearing coincidence

The outer arc terminals sit at (4.87, 16.52) and (17.13, 16.52) with a 0.75pt stroke radius. A 3.00pt lamp on baseline y 18.25 needs 2.25pt of centre distance from them, which frees x 6.31 → 15.69. At pitch 4.50 that is slots at 6.50, 11.00 and 15.50 — three lamps, 8.88pt of free span, and not one point of extra width. The sketch drew three dots; the geometry independently wanted three. From the fourth agent on, the row keeps its origin at x 6.50 and grows to the right.

Optical weight against its neighbours

The battery is 22.5pt of body and the wifi fan is 18pt across. The ring is 18pt in outer diameter, which looks like an overshoot until you notice that a hollow ring reads at its centreline, not its outer edge. The perceived circle here is 16.50pt — within half a point of a 16pt solid glyph, and the standard circular overshoot on top. Ink coverage is far lower than the battery’s, so it sits back rather than forward.

The width is not free

The item grows from 22pt to 55pt as agents spawn, and everything to its left slides. That is a real cost and there is no way around it if one lamp means one agent. The mitigation is that the growth is monotonic and always to the right of a fixed ring, so the ring itself — the thing you actually aim at — never moves relative to the item’s left edge.

The 1x representation

The primary geometry is Retina-first: a 1.50 stroke on a 1.50 counter is a 3.00pt module, and the 1x counter floor is 2.00pt. Rather than pretend, the mark ships as a two-representation NSImage. The 1x rep re-spends the same 18pt on a coarser module and every edge lands on a whole point.

One thing that is genuinely fine at 1x

The notch. Making the 50% tick subtractive instead of additive did not just save radial budget — it moved the only fine detail in the mark to the one location where a circle rasterises perfectly. A 1.00pt vertical slot at the top of a horizontally-tangent arc has two whole-pixel edges at any scale factor. That is the best-rasterising element in the design and it carries the most information.
